\abstract{Researchers routinely test the normality of Likert composite scores to choose between parametric and nonparametric analyses, yet these tests assume continuous data and the composite is discrete. We evaluated 11 normality tests with Monte Carlo simulation (10,000 replications; 3 to 9 response categories, eight sample sizes, five levels of non-normality) and subsampling of six real instruments. Under the nominal criterion ($p<.05$), 8 of the 11 tests rejected up to 100\% of the time with no added non-normality, because of discreteness alone. A decision threshold calibrated on a discrete reference condition removes this distortion; size-adjusted power is then higher with more response categories than with few. D'Agostino-Pearson, Jarque-Bera, and the kurtosis test showed the smallest deviation from the nominal 5\%, and D'Agostino-Pearson had the highest adjusted power. We discuss when a significant normality test on a Likert composite can be trusted and what to do otherwise.}

\keywords{Likert scale, response categories, normality tests, Monte Carlo simulation, severity of non-normality, composite score}
